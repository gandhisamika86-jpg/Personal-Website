Surgical Robot Fatigue Reduction.

▪ Overview
Prototyped features to reduce surgeon fatigue and simplify robot operation during long procedures, now under review by J&J's IP team for a potential patent.
01
Section 1
Problem

Surgical robots put the surgeon fully in the loop for every motion, every incision, every suture, every adjustment routes through the surgeon's own hands at the controls for the full length of the procedure. That control is exactly what makes these systems valuable, but it also means the system inherits every human limitation that comes with hours of sustained, high-precision manual operation.

Dependency

Fully Surgeon-Controlled

Every motion routes through the surgeon manually, the robot has no independent way to reduce the physical and cognitive load of a long case.

Duration

Long-Hour Procedures

Robotic surgeries regularly run for extended stretches, and fatigue accumulating over that time can measurably affect precision and decision-making late in a case.

Training cost

Extensive Ramp-Up Time

Learning to operate these systems well takes a significant investment of a surgeon's time, time that's already the scarcest resource in the room.

02
Section 2
Solution

My innovation centered on prototyping a set of features specifically aimed at the two things surgeons actually feel over the course of a long case: physical fatigue from sustained manual control, and the friction of operating a system that demands as much precision on hour four as it does in the first five minutes. Rather than treating training and fatigue as separate problems, the features were designed to make the system itself easier to pick up and less physically demanding to run, lowering both the learning curve and the cumulative strain of a full procedure.

03
Section 3
Status

The concept has already cleared an internal bar most ideas never reach: I presented the work directly to J&J's Patent Manager, and it's now under active review by the IP team to determine whether it's patentable.
