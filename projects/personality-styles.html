The Art of Working in a Diverse Team: Understanding People's Communication Preference

▪ Core Idea
The “secret sauce” to working with different people is understanding how they prefer to communicate—and being versatile enough to meet them there.
OverviewFour StylesCross-Style CollaborationDriver ExampleTakeaway
01

The Lesson

Flex the Approach, Not the Message

During MTDE 313/613, guest speaker Mr. Joe Small explained that one of the most important skills in working with different people is recognizing their personality and communication style. The value is not in labeling people; it is in understanding how they prefer to receive information and adapting the way I approach them.

That versatility can make technical discussions, project decisions, and disagreements more productive. I may communicate naturally in one style, but effective collaboration requires me to recognize when another person needs a different pace, level of detail, or tone.

Understanding another person’s style helps me communicate the same idea in the way they are most likely to hear it.
02

Four Communication Styles

Results & Action

Driver

Direct, decisive, fast-moving, and focused on outcomes, efficiency, and control.

Energy & Vision

Expressive

Enthusiastic, persuasive, people-oriented, and motivated by ideas, interaction, and the big picture.

Facts & Logic

Analytical

Precise, deliberate, cautious, and focused on evidence, structure, accuracy, and clear reasoning.

Trust & Support

Amiable

Patient, cooperative, relationship-oriented, and focused on agreement, stability, and supporting others.

03

Cross-Style Collaboration

The quadrants do not define who can or cannot work together. A Driver can collaborate effectively with an Amiable, Expressive, or Analytical colleague when both people recognize their differences and adjust how they communicate. Cross-quadrant pairings can be especially complementary: one person may create momentum while another builds trust, brings energy, or protects the quality of the decision.

The Practical Goal

Instead of expecting everyone to communicate like me, I should identify what the other person values—speed, enthusiasm, evidence, or reassurance—and frame the conversation accordingly. The objective is not to change my conclusion; it is to make the reasoning accessible to the person receiving it.

04

Example: Communicating with a Driver

Concise, Supportive, and Decision-Oriented

If I am speaking with someone who has a Driver style, I should respect their preference for speed and results. That means leading with the conclusion, keeping my points concise, explaining the decision required, and showing that I am prepared to support and execute the chosen direction.

Flexibility Does Not Mean Silence

I should still put my opinions on the table. The difference is in delivery: I would state my view calmly, briefly, and with supporting evidence—more as a clear recommendation than a confrontation. Adapting my tone does not weaken my point; it increases the chance that the point is considered.

01

Lead with the answer

Start with the outcome, recommendation, or decision needed before providing background.

02

Use concise points

Remove unnecessary detail and organize the discussion around results, risks, and next actions.

03

Respect decision ownership

Be supportive of the final decision and show willingness to move quickly once the direction is set.

04

Offer disagreement constructively

Present an alternative softly but clearly, using evidence and a recommended path rather than an emotional challenge.

05

My Takeaway

Versatility is not becoming a different person. It is learning how to make my ideas understandable, useful, and actionable for different people.
