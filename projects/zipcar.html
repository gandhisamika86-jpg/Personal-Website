An Analysis of ZipCar's Leadership and Strategy

▪ Overview
An analysis of Robin Chase’s leadership and Keefer Welsh’s fit as president of Zipcar, exploring resourcefulness, stakeholder communication, and hiring for a startup’s actual needs.

Analysis by Samika Gandhi. Based on Robin Chase, Zipcar, and an Inconvenient Discovery by Deborah Ancona and Cate Reavis, MIT Sloan Management, July 25, 2014 (case 14-153). The assessments below present my interpretation of the case.

01

Analysis Questions

My response addresses two questions: why Keefer Welsh was the wrong hire for Zipcar, and how Robin Chase’s strengths and weaknesses shaped her leadership.

02

Why Keefer Welsh Was the Wrong Hire

Keefer Welsh was a poor choice for president of ZipCar. Two failures stand out: an industry mismatch and a lack of fit with the company’s actual needs.

Industry Mismatch

Welsh came from hospitality, an industry that spends heavily to keep clients happy and returning to the same ambience and luxury every time. That mindset clashes with a startup, where frugality is essential and every unnecessary expense threatens survival. The board appears to have valued polish and experience over whether his habits fit a company with no money to spare.

The Wrong Person for the Role

Welsh had no experience in car-sharing or in anything close to technology, and he had no clear vision for where to take ZipCar. According to Chase, he was hired largely because the board doubted that a female CEO could keep up. Welsh was the wrong person, at the wrong time, for the wrong company.

03

Robin Chase as a Leader

Robin Chase is an exceptional leader. She combines persistence, resourcefulness, strategic hiring, clear communication, and honest emotional transparency, though she also has a few notable flaws.

Persistence

Chase was relentlessly persistent. People told her that car-sharing would never work and pointed to a long history of failures, yet she chose to tune out the noise and keep going. She networked heavily with stakeholders across Boston, including parking lot owners, environmental policymakers, and investors. Many of those connections were dead ends, but she kept at it, and that persistence is how she found the owner of Communauto in Montreal and, later, her investors, even amid the dot-com bust.

Resourcefulness and Strategic Team-Building

Chase was both frugal and strategic in building her team. She brought in her brother as business developer, her husband to build the technology, and her next-door neighbor Stephen to run operations. Stephen’s background as a nurse assistant and restaurant worker made him well suited to operations, and he, along with Larry, was paid hourly to conserve cash. Larry, an engineer by training and a cyclist by hobby, was given a task most people would find mundane, moving cars between locations, which matched two things he loved. She also drew on her husband’s electrical engineering expertise to build exactly the technology she envisioned. When necessary, she was willing to make hard calls, such as removing her co-founder Danielson because of her part-time commitment to the venture.

Vision

Chase had a novel vision: pairing wireless technology with car-sharing. This became one of ZipCar’s key differentiators and was central to the company surviving in the industry.

Communication and Stakeholder Management

Chase’s ability to communicate may be her most distinctive strength. She made win-win pitches that stakeholders found hard to refuse. For example, she told Volkswagen that her business was not a threat but a test drive for customers before they committed to buying a car. She also sent regular email updates to customers on the company’s progress. This made customers feel valued, since a senior executive was reaching out to them, and part of a broader community. It also gave her direct feedback: she learned that customers disliked the two-tiered pricing model and changed it accordingly.

Emotional Transparency

Chase was open about the emotional weight of leading. When she discovered that a modeling error meant the company was earning only half the expected revenue, she locked herself in the bedroom of her home, where the team worked, and cried for two hours. This gave her team a genuine signal of the stakes and of what she was going through.

Problem-Solving

Chase’s other traits come together in her problem-solving. When choosing a company name, she narrowed her options to three and asked random people on the street which one stuck in their memory, allowing her to choose with confidence. When she realized her pricing model had been miscalculated, she identified the metric that was working, daily revenue, and raised it by 20% to close the gap. Because she communicated openly with customers, she was able to retain them through the change.

04

Leadership Limitations

Chase also had clear weaknesses:

  • Perfectionism: She spent too much time trying to build the perfect pricing model before launch. When the wireless unlocking technology was still a month from being ready, she refused to delay the launch, though by her own admission, a delay of a couple of months would not have hurt the business in the long run.
  • Difficulty maintaining relationships: Beyond the conflict with Danielson, Chase herself was pushed off the board, which reflects a weakness in sustaining key relationships.
